Packet Fragmentation: A Firewall Bypass Technique

Understanding how fragmented packets can slip past traditional firewalls.

What is Packet Fragmentation?

Normally, when a large data packet needs to be sent across a network, it's broken down into smaller pieces (fragments) if its size exceeds the Maximum Transmission Unit (MTU) of a network segment. These fragments are then reassembled at the destination.

Attackers exploit this mechanism by intentionally fragmenting malicious payloads into very small, unusual sizes to evade detection.

The Attacker's Intent

An attacker has a malicious payload (e.g., an exploit, malware signature) that they want to deliver to a target system protected by a firewall.

Intentional Fragmentation

The attacker intentionally fragments the payload into numerous, very small packets. Each fragment might be so small that, individually, it doesn't contain enough data to trigger a security alert or match a known signature on a basic firewall.

Firewall Encounter (Traditional/Basic)

The fragmented packets arrive at a traditional or improperly configured firewall. These firewalls often inspect packets one by one, looking for specific signatures or anomalies.

Traditional Firewall Logic: "This tiny packet looks harmless, allow!"

↓
4
Bypass Achieved

Since each fragment individually appears benign or lacks a full signature, the firewall fails to detect the threat and allows all fragments to pass through to the target system.

The malicious payload bypasses the firewall undetected!

↓
5
Reassembly and Execution

On the target system, the operating system or application reassembles all the small fragments back into the original, complete malicious payload. This payload can then execute, compromise the system, or establish a foothold for the attacker.

Reassembled Malicious Payload: ACTIVATED!

The Solution: Next-Generation Firewalls (NGFW)

To combat packet fragmentation attacks, organizations need more advanced firewalls. Next-Generation Firewalls (NGFWs) are designed to:

  • Reassemble Packets: They maintain a state table and reassemble all fragmented packets into their original form before inspection.
  • Perform Deep Packet Inspection (DPI): They inspect the entire reassembled payload, not just individual fragments or headers, to detect malicious content.
  • Identify Anomalies: They can spot unusual fragmentation patterns (e.g., excessively small fragments or fragments out of order) as indicators of potential attack.
  • Drop Invalid Fragments: They can be configured to drop any malformed or suspicious fragmented packets.

Ensure your firewall is properly configured and up-to-date to prevent these types of evasion techniques!
