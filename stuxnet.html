The Stuxnet Cyberattack

How the world’s first digital weapon sabotaged Iran’s nuclear program.

Step 1: Intelligence Gathering

US/Israel intelligence agencies study Iranian centrifuges, reportedly acquiring samples and blueprints via the supply chain.

Step 2: Identifying Weakness

The specific Siemens PLC (Programmable Logic Controller) systems controlling the centrifuges at the Natanz facility are identified as the key target.

Step 3: Planning Covert Sabotage

A sophisticated piece of malware (Stuxnet) is designed to subtly alter centrifuge operations without alerting operators.

Step 4: Infiltration via Contractors

The malware spreads via infected USB drives, likely from third-party contractors, bypassing the "air-gapped" security of the Natanz facility.

Step 5: Payload Execution

Stuxnet records normal operational data from the PLCs and then plays it back, all while manipulating centrifuge rotor speeds—speeding them up and slowing them down.

Step 6: Physical Damage

The intense physical stress causes centrifuges to fail and break. Operators see "all normal" on their control screens while the facility is being destroyed.

Step 7: Global Discovery

The malware accidentally leaks from Natanz and spreads globally. Cybersecurity firm Symantec identifies it, noting its use of multiple (four) zero-day exploits.

Step 8: Attribution

The attack is widely attributed to the US and Israel ("Operation Olympic Games"), marking the first major use of cyber-physical warfare.

Key Impact

  • ~1,000 Centrifuges Destroyed
  • Iran's Nuclear Program Delayed Significantly
  • Recognized as the First Digital Weapon in History
